Computed Crystal Energy Landscapes for Understanding and Predicting Organic Crystal Structures and Polymorphism
Sarah L. Price  (2008, 361 citations)

Why it's foundational: This account defines the concept that anchors modern molecular CSP: the computed crystal energy landscape. It frames polymorphism as a molecule's ability to adopt multiple crystal structures and argues that the goal of CSP is not merely finding a single global minimum but mapping the set of low-energy structures that a molecule can plausibly form. For a researcher using GA/evolutionary search on molecular crystals, this is the conceptual target that any search algorithm is trying to reconstruct, and it explains why reliable relative lattice energies matter as much as efficient sampling.

How it connects: The crystal energy landscape idea underpins essentially all contemporary molecular CSP work, including the blind tests, dispersion-corrected DFT ranking, and machine-learned energy models represented elsewhere in this list. GA and evolutionary methods (and their PSO relatives) are precisely the sampling engines used to populate such landscapes for molecular systems; understanding what a landscape is and what features distinguish real polymorphs from artifacts tells you what your evolutionary search must produce and how to judge success. It links directly to later work on interpreting landscapes, predicting late-appearing polymorphs, and combining CSP with experimental screening.

Take away: Internalize that CSP is the generation and interpretation of a crystal energy landscape (the ensemble of low-energy packings), not a hunt for one structure; the value lies in understanding and predicting polymorphism, which sets the accuracy and sampling demands on any evolutionary search method.
