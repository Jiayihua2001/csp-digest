📚 Classic of the day — build your canon
The prediction of inorganic crystal structures using a genetic algorithm and energy minimisation
Scott M. Woodley, Peter D. Battle, Julian D. Gale et al.  (1999, 353 citations)

Why it's foundational: This paper is one of the earliest and most influential demonstrations that a genetic algorithm coupled with energy minimisation can predict crystal structures from minimal input (unit cell dimensions and constituent elements). It established the core evolutionary paradigm for CSP, showing that a GA could successfully reproduce a large set of known binary oxides and various ternary oxide structure types (Perovskite, Pyrochlore, Spinel). For a researcher focused on GA/evolutionary CSP methods, this is a genuine cornerstone that predates and underpins later evolutionary and swarm-based approaches.

How it connects: It is the direct methodological ancestor of modern evolutionary CSP tools. The GA-plus-local-relaxation recipe it introduced is echoed in later particle-swarm (paper 2), adaptive GA (paper 38), and interface GA (paper 40) methods, and the same global-optimization mindset appears across the broader landscape (paper 5). Its lineage connects to Holland's foundational GA theory (paper 23) on one side and to today's machine-learning-accelerated and DFT-coupled CSP workflows (papers 38, 48) on the other. Understanding this work clarifies why evolution operators, population-based search, and energy minimisation became the standard scaffolding for structure prediction, including the molecular-crystal domain the researcher targets.

Take away: The central idea to internalize: CSP can be framed as a global optimization problem where a genetic algorithm generates candidate structures that are then locally relaxed via energy minimisation, and this evolutionary search can rediscover known structure types from only composition and cell information without prior structural assumptions.
