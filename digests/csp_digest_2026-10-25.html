ROY revisited, again: the eighth solved structure
Mélissa Tan, Alexander G. Shtukenberg, Shengcai Zhu et al.  (2018, 70 citations)

Why it's foundational: This paper is the most directly on-target cornerstone for a researcher working on molecular crystal structure prediction. It demonstrates the use of crystal structure prediction (CSP) algorithms in synergy with experimental X-ray powder diffraction to solve a real, previously unknown polymorph of ROY, a canonical polymorphism benchmark molecule. ROY (5-methyl-2-[(2-nitrophenyl)amino]-3-thiophenecarbonitrile) is famous as one of the most polymorphic organic compounds known, making it the standard stress test for CSP methods. The abstract shows CSP generating candidate structures that are validated against diffraction data to establish a genuine new solved structure, illustrating the full predict-then-confirm workflow that defines modern molecular CSP.

How it connects: Modern molecular CSP rests on generating large sets of candidate packings and ranking them by lattice energy, exactly the kind of landscape exploration that genetic-algorithm and evolutionary search methods are designed to perform efficiently. ROY is the benchmark against which search algorithms (including GA/evolutionary samplers) and energy ranking schemes are routinely measured, because its many polymorphs probe whether a method can both find the right structures and rank them correctly. This work connects the computational search side to experimental validation, which is the loop that current CSP research, blind tests, and machine-learning-accelerated energy evaluation all aim to tighten.

Take away: Internalize that successful molecular CSP is a coupled search-and-rank problem validated against experiment: an algorithm must thoroughly sample the configurational landscape to find candidate polymorphs and then rank them accurately, with highly polymorphic systems like ROY serving as the definitive proving ground for any new search method, including genetic and evolutionary approaches.
