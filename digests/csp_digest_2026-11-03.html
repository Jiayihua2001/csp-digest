📚 Landmark of the day — AI for Science landmark
Mastering the game of Go without human knowledge
David Silver, Julian Schrittwieser, Karen Simonyan et al.  (2017, 9075 citations, Nature)

Worth 30 minutes? AlphaGo Zero showed that one network, trained only by self-play with tree search as a policy-improvement operator, could surpass systems built on human expert data. That made search plus learned evaluation, improving itself in a loop, a general recipe. It underlies AlphaZero and MuZero, AlphaTensor and AlphaDev, and many search-guided design methods in chemistry and materials.

What it could inspire: Treat a GA or Genarris pipeline as search that a learned prior could guide. A policy could propose mutations, crossovers or space-group and cell moves, while a value network estimates the final DFT rank. Both would be retrained on the search's own outcomes, closing the loop. DFT ranking becomes the reward, replacing a hand-tuned operator mix and fitness-sharing heuristics.

Foundations to learn: Monte Carlo tree search with PUCT selection, which balances a prior, visit counts and value estimates. Policy iteration: search visit distributions serve as improved policy targets, and game outcomes serve as value targets. Joint policy-value training with a residual network. The exploration-exploitation tradeoff. Why bootstrapping from self-generated data avoids the ceiling of imitating human or expert data.

30-minute reading guide: Spend 10 min on the main text describing the self-play reinforcement learning loop and its MCTS schematic figure. Spend 10 min on the Methods sections covering search (select, expand and evaluate, backup, play) and the loss function. Spend 5 min on the training-curve and ablation figures (separate vs dual networks, supervised vs self-play). Skip the Go joseki analysis and the hardware details. Use the last 5 min to map the components onto your GA.
