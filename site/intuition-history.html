Role of Dispersion Interactions in the Polymorphism and Entropic Stabilization of the Aspirin Crystal (2014) · doi:10.1103/physrevlett.113.055701

Core idea: Aspirin forms I and II are degenerate in lattice energy (within ~0.1 kJ/mol), so lattice-energy ranking cannot explain why form I dominates. Including many-body dispersion (MBD) in both the lattice energy AND the phonons reveals an entropic, finite-temperature mechanism: MBD softens low-frequency phonons in form I (via plasmon-phonon coupling), raising its vibrational entropy and making it thermodynamically more stable at room temperature by ~2.56 kJ/mol.

Innovation: First demonstration that many-body vdW interactions affect not just lattice energies but lattice vibrations, uncovering a coupling between collective electronic (plasmon) fluctuations and low-frequency phonons that shifts frequencies down. Prior work treated the polymorphs as degenerate and invoked kinetics; this provides a thermodynamic, entropy-driven explanation and also rules out mechanical instability of form II.

Accurate and Efficient Method for Many-Body van der Waals Interactions (2012) · doi:10.1103/physrevlett.108.236402

Core idea: The paper introduces the many-body dispersion (MBD) method: it augments the Tkatchenko-Scheffler atom-in-molecule vdW scheme with a classical self-consistent screening (SCS) equation to capture long-range electrostatic (de)polarization, then computes the fully nonadditive vdW energy to infinite order via coupled quantum harmonic oscillators (CFDM). Screening and many-body effects are shown to matter even for small molecules and become crucial for biomolecule conformers and molecular crystals.

Innovation: Prior vdW corrections neglected long-range electrostatic screening and truncated many-body effects at pairwise (or three-body Axilrod-Teller). This work adds SCS screening to effective atomic polarizabilities and solves a coupled-oscillator Hamiltonian for the vdW energy to infinite order in the dipole approximation, all at negligible computational cost, with a single physically motivated range-separation parameter per functional.

Pairwise-additive dispersion overestimates polarizability and C6 in large/extended systems because it ignores long-range electrostatic screening (depolarization) from distant fluctuating dipoles; self-consistent screening corrects this.

Accurate Molecular Van Der Waals Interactions from Ground-State Electron Density and Free-Atom Reference Data (2009) · doi:10.1103/physrevlett.102.073005

Core idea: This paper introduces the TS (Tkatchenko-Scheffler) van der Waals method: a parameter-free way to compute pairwise C6 dispersion coefficients and vdW radii for atoms in molecules by rescaling accurate free-atom reference values (Chu-Dalgarno) using the ratio of the atom's Hirshfeld-partitioned effective volume in the molecule to its free-atom volume. Effective atomic polarizabilities, and hence C6 coefficients, thus respond automatically to the local bonding environment from the ground-state electron density.

Innovation: Prior density-based C6 schemes had 15-20% average errors (up to 40-60%) because DFT polarizabilities are grossly overestimated; earlier C6/R6 corrections (Grimme) fit parameters empirically to experimental C6 or post-HF binding energies. TS instead uses the electron density only to compute the RELATIVE polarizability (effective/free-atom volume ratio), keeping absolute accuracy from free-atom references, cutting the C6 error to 5.5% and making the coefficients depend explicitly on hybridization/bonding without fitting.

☆
The TS method reproduces intermolecular C6 coefficients with a mean absolute relative error of 5.5% (4.5% if H2 is excluded) against 1225 experimental DOSD-derived pairs, a factor of 2-3 better than prior ground-state-density C6 methods (which had 15-20% average, 40-60% max errors).
statistic · scope: 8 atoms and 42 small-to-medium organic/inorganic molecules (up to C8H18), Z'=1 gas-phase geometries · established
'mean absolute error in the C6 coefficients is 5.5%... for 1225 intermolecular pairs'; 'if the H2 molecule is excluded... the MARE drops down to 4.5%'; existing methods errors '15%-20% on average and maximum deviation of 40%-60%'
