How many more polymorphs of ROY remain undiscovered
Gregory J. O. Beran, Isaac J. Sugden, Chandler Greenwell et al.  (2021, 104 citations)

Why it's foundational: This paper centers on ROY, the benchmark compound that holds the record for the largest number of fully characterized organic crystal polymorphs, and asks the defining question of CSP: how many polymorphs can exist and how many remain undiscovered. For a researcher in molecular CSP this frames the core scientific challenge that any prediction method, including genetic/evolutionary approaches, must confront - enumerating and ranking the many low-energy packing arrangements a flexible molecule can adopt. ROY is the canonical stress test for the whole field, so understanding it builds the mental scaffolding against which methods are judged.

How it connects: Modern molecular CSP workflows are validated precisely on polymorph-rich systems like ROY, where the energy landscape is densely populated with competing structures. Genetic-algorithm and evolutionary search methods exist to explore that landscape efficiently, generating candidate packings and evolving toward low-energy minima; the ROY problem defines what a successful global search must recover. The abstract notes that four of its forms were reported since 2019, underscoring that experimental discovery and computational prediction co-evolve, which is exactly the loop GA-based CSP feeds into. It connects naturally to companion CSP work (e.g. the ROY form R05 solved via CSP algorithms in this same list) and to lattice-energy ranking methods used to score GA-generated structures.

Take away: Internalize that a single molecule can have an unexpectedly large and still-growing number of near-degenerate crystal polymorphs, so the goal of CSP is not one structure but a ranked landscape of many - the measure of a search method (GA/evolutionary included) is how completely and accurately it recovers that dense set of low-energy forms.
